Mass fluorography was introduced in the USSR in the 1940s, and across the region it still runs on Soviet-era mandates 30. In Kazakhstan, a yearly X-ray is mandatory for listed groups: health, education and food workers, students, the military, people arriving to live or work (including labour migrants), and risk groups such as TB contacts and prisoners 31.

The social-science question

Kazakhstan already has the X-ray machines and the queues. What it lacks is enough people to read the images, a way to reach those who never get X-rayed, and a public rule for how many missed cases are acceptable. Would AI help the people this system misses, or mainly make an old institution cheaper to run?

What the numbers show, and what they hide

Official statistics count the TB cases the system finds. The WHO estimates how many cases there really are. The gap between the two numbers is people who were never diagnosed or never registered.

The missing people

What the data doesn't tell us: who the 5.6 million screened people are, and who never gets screened. On paper, even labour migrants must be X-rayed every year 31, but nobody publishes how many actually are. There are also no public figures on how many cases human readers miss, or how many X-ray machines still use film.

!
My position on mass screening

X-raying more than a quarter of the country every year finds one case per 1,400 images. I would keep chest X-ray but stop treating it as a yearly ritual for everyone: shift effort to the risk groups already named in Kazakhstan's own rules and to people who never come in 31, as the World Bank and WHO recommend for Belarus 30. AI makes this affordable because a mobile unit no longer needs a radiologist on board.

The bill: what it would really cost

LAB REPORTPatient: AI fluorography, Kazakhstan · sample: 5.6M scans/yr
TestLevel = acceptableFlag
Environmentcompute ≈ 47–470 kWh/yr (my estimate: 0.1 s on a 300 W GPU per image) on a grid that is ~75% coal 22. Water use follows electricity and cooling 37, so it is small for the AI itself. Storing ~56 TB of new images a year (my estimate, ~10 MB each) plus backups will use more power and water than the AI; not measured.
normal
Moneylicences ≈ $1.25M/yr if 500 machines × $2,500, with installation, training and updates included; Kazakhstan is eligible 23. "500 machines" is my assumption. Not priced: digitising film machines, servers in Kazakhstan, a local validation study, the audit. The money goes to Delft Imaging in the Netherlands 35, while a Kazakh CT AI waits for a tariff 21.
↑ borderline
Labourradiologists, already in short supply 29, become auditors of AI scores; junior doctors read fewer normal images and lose practice; the models rest on hidden data work such as image labelling and verification 36; doctors may over-trust the score.
↑ high
Power & lawunder Law 230-VIII the owner classifies the system; a medical AI would likely be high-risk, with an audit to enter the "trusted" list 2425 (rules in force since 26 Apr 2026: sectoral bodies, here the Ministry of Health, publish lists of trusted high-risk AI; the owner applies with a positive audit conclusion 3832). The EU AI Act treats AI in medical devices as high-risk and requires human oversight 33; UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation keeps ultimate responsibility with humans 34. Data must stay in KZ 26. A national lung register could be misused to exclude people.
↑ high
Data ethicsthe yearly X-ray is mandatory for many groups 31, but nobody agreed to train an AI with it; medical data is sensitive and needs explicit consent 27; accuracy is lower for older and ex-TB patients 121319.
↑↑ critical

How this was made

Made together with Claude (Anthropic). Claude suggested possible topics; I chose and narrowed the focus to AI reading lung X-rays and brought my own material, including an article and a PDF pitch deck about the Kazakh startup Cortex AI. Claude helped with research, calculations and coding the page. We shaped the design together.

What I rejected. Claude first recommended air pollution in Almaty as the safest topic; I chose lung X-rays instead. Claude's first version of the site was long, text-heavy and full of tables; I rejected it and asked for a visual, Dear Data-style page with less text. I also rejected the traffic-light verdict and the receipt, because they looked like other projects; we replaced them with an X-ray film and a lab report.

What I accepted. Treating Cortex AI as a case study rather than a source of facts, and a full source check that removed several WHO figures I could not confirm.

I did not use Cortex AI's 94.1% claim as fact, because it has no published validation. Every number links to a source below, and assumptions are labelled.
